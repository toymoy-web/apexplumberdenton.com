A town named for a lake that is no longer on the map

The name is older than the reservoir most people picture. The City of Dallas finished Garza Dam in 1927, and the water behind it was called Lake Dallas. When the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers completed Lewisville Dam in 1955, the old dam was breached in 1957 and Lake Dallas became part of Lewisville Lake.

That history still shapes the town. Streets run downhill toward the shoreline, older homes sit near the water while newer subdivisions climb away from it, and low lots collect runoff during North Texas downpours.

For plumbing, low ground near a lake means two recurring calls: sewer lines that back up when heavy rain soaks the soil, and drainage pits or crawlspaces that need a working pump. Our sump and ejector pump service and lateral repairs cover both.

After a heavy rain near the lake: what to check

Saturated ground near Lewisville Lake shows plumbing weak spots quickly. Walk through these after a big storm.

  • Run water in the lowest tub or shower and watch for slow draining or gurgling, which can point to rain water in the sewer lateral.
  • Make sure the outdoor cleanout cap is on and unbroken, so storm water cannot pour straight into the line.
  • Check that downspouts carry roof water away from the house and are not tied into the sewer.
  • If you have a drainage pit or crawlspace pump, confirm it cycled and that its discharge line is clear.
  • Look for soggy strips of lawn that stay wet after the rest of the yard dries, a sign of a leaking water or sewer line.

The International Plumbing Code (IPC), the model code most North Texas cities adopt, keeps storm water out of sewers meant only for sewage, so a downspout tied into the lateral is a code problem as well as a backup risk.
