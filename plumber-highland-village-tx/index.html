Attic water heaters and two-story homes

Builders of two-story houses here often tucked the water heater up in the attic or an upstairs closet. When a tank up there fails, gravity sends the water straight through the ceiling below.

A properly installed attic heater rests in a catch pan with its own line running outdoors or to a drain. Those pans and lines age too. A cracked pan or a clogged pan drain is how a slow drip becomes a stained ceiling.

If your heater lives upstairs and is past about ten years old, it is worth pricing a planned replacement before it fails. Water heater replacement covers attic installs, drain pans and the permit side.

Two-story homes change the order of priorities. Start here.

Close the main

The house valve is usually in the garage, often near the water heater closet. If it will not turn, the meter box near the street is the backup.

Water from an upstairs leak can reach ceiling lights and fans below. Switch those circuits off at the breaker before going near them.

What to do when an attic water heater leaks

These steps limit ceiling damage while help is on the way.

  • Close the cold water valve on top of the heater, if you can reach it safely.
  • Switch off the heater's breaker, or set a gas unit's control to pilot.
  • Put buckets and towels under any ceiling spots that are dripping or bulging.
  • Do not stand under a sagging ceiling. Poke a small hole at its lowest point to let water drain into a bucket, only if it is safe to do so.
  • Call the 24/7 line and mention the heater is in the attic, so the plumber brings the right equipment.

The International Plumbing Code (IPC), the model code North Texas cities adopt, calls for a drain pan wherever a leaking heater would damage the building, which covers nearly every attic install.
