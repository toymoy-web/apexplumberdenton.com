Sump pump installation: where Denton homes actually use them

A sump pump is an electric pump in a pit that collects groundwater or drainage and pushes it out and away from the house. In markets with basements, it sits in the basement floor. In Denton, most homes are slab-on-grade, so pumps show up in different places.

The most common is a drainage pit tied to a French drain or yard drain, often installed alongside foundation work. Denton's expansive clay holds water, and a drain that collects runoff near the slab needs a pump when there is no downhill route to let it flow away by gravity.

Older pier-and-beam homes near downtown and in the Oak-Hickory area sometimes have a pump in the crawlspace to keep standing water from collecting under the floor. And some homes with a below-grade bathroom or a garage-level laundry use a sewage ejector pump, a related but different device that lifts wastewater up to the sewer line.

Local plumbers and foundation contractors across Denton offer sump installation for exactly these reasons, even though basements are rare here.

Emergency sump pump repair during heavy rain

A sump pump that hums but does not pump, runs nonstop or has stopped while water rises in the pit is an emergency during a storm.

Check the breaker and the GFCI outlet first. If the pump still will not run, keep power cords off the wet floor and call the 24/7 emergency line.

The plumber repairs or replaces the pump, checks the float and check valve and confirms the discharge line carries water away from the foundation.

Pump failed during a storm? Do this first

Water and electricity are the main risks. Work through these in order.

  • Stay out of standing water near the pump. Switch off its circuit at the breaker only if you can reach the panel from a dry spot.
  • Check the obvious: is the pump plugged in, has a GFCI outlet tripped, has the breaker tripped?
  • Look at the float switch. A float stuck against the pit wall is the most common reason a working pump does not start.
  • If water keeps rising, move belongings away and photograph the area for insurance.
  • Call the 24/7 line. If sewage is involved, as with an ejector pump, stop using water in the house.

Where the water is allowed to go

A sump pump that collects groundwater and rain must not discharge into the sanitary sewer. The International Plumbing Code (IPC), which Denton adopted in its 2021 edition, keeps storm and groundwater out of sewers meant for sewage only. Extra rainwater in the sewer is one of the reasons mains back up during storms.

Instead, the discharge should run to daylight, a dry well or an approved storm drainage point, far enough from the foundation that the water does not soak back toward the slab. On clay soil, a discharge that dumps right next to the house can undo the point of having a pump.

Sewage ejector pumps are the opposite case. They carry wastewater and must connect to the sanitary drain, with a sealed, vented basin. Mixing up the two is a common mistake when older setups are modified.

Signs your pump needs service before the next storm

Test the pump in spring and fall, before the heavy rain seasons. Pour a bucket of water into the pit and watch it cycle.

  • The pump is more than about ten years old.
  • It makes grinding, rattling or loud humming noises.
  • It runs even when the weather has been dry, which can point to a hidden water source such as a leaking supply line.
  • Rust, sludge or debris has built up in the pit.
  • There is no backup, such as a battery-powered secondary pump, in a spot where failure would flood living space.

A pump that runs in dry weather is worth a meter test. A leak check on the supply lines shows how to rule out a leaking water line as the source.

What sump pump work costs depend on

These factors decide most of the price.

  • Repair versus replacement, and the pump type: submersible or pedestal.
  • Adding a battery backup or an alarm.
  • Digging a new pit versus replacing a pump in an existing one.
  • Discharge line length and where it can legally drain.
  • Emergency timing during a storm versus a scheduled visit.

Some homeowner policies only cover water from a failed sump pump if a specific endorsement was added. Check yours before storm season.
